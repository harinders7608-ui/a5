Kinetic Tribology of Co-Axial Escapements vs Swiss Lever Radial Friction

By Elena Rostova, Senior Escapement Architect • Published March 10, 2026 • 14 Min Read

The Tribological Bottleneck of the Swiss Lever Escapement

For over two centuries, the Swiss lever escapement has served as the dominant regulating mechanism in mechanical horology. Invented by Thomas Mudge in 1755, the Swiss lever is remarkably reliable and self-starting. However, from a mechanical engineering perspective, it suffers from a fundamental tribological flaw: sliding friction.

As the escape wheel teeth interact with the synthetic ruby pallet stones, the tooth tip must slide across the flat inclined impulse plane of the pallet jewel over an angle of approximately ten degrees. This sliding motion generates substantial shear friction, requiring fluid boundary lubrication.

Over three to five years, synthetic horological oils inevitably oxidize, polymerize, and migrate away from the pallet faces. As friction increases, the amplitude of the balance wheel drops precipitously, destroying the watch's rate stability and demanding immediate caliber disassembly and cleaning.

Dr. George Daniels & The Co-Axial Breakthrough

In 1974, legendary British independent watchmaker Dr. George Daniels resolved this two-century-old dilemma by inventing the Co-Axial escapement. Daniels recognized that if the impulse could be delivered via tangential pushing contact rather than sliding radial contact, sliding friction could be virtually eliminated.

The Daniels co-axial escapement utilizes a complex system comprising an intermediate escape pinion, a double co-axial escape wheel with stepped teeth, and an anchor with three synthetic ruby pallet jewels. Impulse is delivered directly to the balance roller in both clockwise and counter-clockwise directions.

Because the escape teeth push tangentially against the pallet stones rather than sliding across them, frictional drag is reduced by over eighty percent. The mechanism functions with minimal lubrication, ensuring steady balance amplitude across decade-long intervals.

Because the mass of the balance wheel is concentrated along its outer perimeter rim, the moment of rotational inertia remains remarkably immune to sudden kinetic wrist impulses encountered during intense alpine ascents or maritime navigation.
